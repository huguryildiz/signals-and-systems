Signals and
Systems

A signal carries information in the way it changes. A system is anything that takes one signal and returns another. This course is the study of what a system does to a signal, and of the one change of variable — into frequency — that turns a hard question into an easy one.

Where the course arrives

Sample too slowly and you get a different signal

The screen beside this shows one cosine in cyan, the instants at which it is sampled in amber, and the signal a receiver rebuilds from those samples alone in green. The sampling rate drifts. Nothing else on the screen changes.

ωs > 2ωM

While the rate stays above twice the highest frequency in the signal, the green curve sits exactly on the cyan one and you cannot see two curves. Below it they separate: the samples are still correct, but they now fit a slower cosine just as well, and the rebuilt signal drops to that frequency instead. The readout turns red at the same instant, because the picture and the inequality are one statement.

Every figure in the course is drawn from the mathematics beside it, and every number a solution states is re-derived by a program that computes it a second way.

The eight modules

One question, asked eight ways

A system is given a signal and returns another one. Every module is either a way of describing the signal, a way of describing what the system does to it, or the change of variable that makes the second question a multiplication instead of an integral.

  1. 0

    What a signal is

    The frame of the course: what is being studied, in continuous and in discrete time.

  2. 1

    Signals and their measures

    Energy, power, time transformations, periodicity, impulses and complex exponentials.

  3. 2

    System properties

    Memory, causality, stability, linearity, time invariance — and a counterexample each.

  4. 3

    LTI systems and convolution

    Why the impulse response is the whole system, and how to slide one signal past another.

  5. 4

    Fourier series

    A periodic signal as a sum of harmonics, in continuous and in discrete time.

  6. 5

    The continuous-time transform

    The same idea for signals that never repeat, with the properties that make it usable.

  7. 6

    The discrete-time transform

    The sequence version, periodic in frequency, and where the two theories differ.

  8. 7

    Sampling and aliasing

    What is kept when a signal is measured at instants, what is lost, and when.
